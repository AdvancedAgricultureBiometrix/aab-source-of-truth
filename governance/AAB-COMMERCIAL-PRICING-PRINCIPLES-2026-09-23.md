# AAB Commercial Pricing Principles — 2026-09-23

**Status:** GOVERNANCE RECORD — COMMERCIAL DESIGN
**Authority:** ESTABLISHES PRICING PRINCIPLES AND METHODOLOGY. Does not establish
a price list. Does not commit AAB to any specific price point. Does not grant
commercial, production, commissioning or regulatory authority.

## The honest starting position

AAB is currently a governed design and demonstration platform — not an
operational EUDR compliance product. The first price is therefore for a bounded
institutional discovery, co-design or controlled pilot — not for an assured
compliance licence.

No production licence should be priced until AAB is implemented, admitted,
behaviourally proven, and has completed at least one paid controlled pilot that
provides real delivery cost and customer value data.

## The governing rule

> We determine the price through a paid, bounded pilot that measures AAB's real
> delivery cost and the customer's measurable value. Until AAB is implemented and
> proven, we sell discovery and validation — not an operational compliance licence.

## How to determine the price — four calculations together

### 1. Establish the true cost floor

For each engagement, calculate the fully loaded cost:

- Country-environment infrastructure
- Configuration and onboarding labour
- Evidence migration and cleaning
- Mapping and satellite-data costs
- Security and sovereignty work
- Training
- Support
- Legal and insurance costs
- Independent scientific or regulatory review
- Third-party provider costs
- Contingency
- Ongoing maintenance

Then apply a sustainable margin:

```
Minimum sustainable price = fully loaded cost ÷ (1 − target margin)
```

Example: if a project costs AUD 60,000 and the target gross margin is 40%:

```
60,000 ÷ 0.60 = AUD 100,000
```

AUD 100,000 — not AUD 60,000 — is the minimum sustainable price. The margin
funds ongoing development, governance, and the next deployment.

### 2. Measure the customer's present problem

Ask the institution:

- How many exporters, suppliers, smallholders, plots and shipments are involved?
- What do they currently spend on consultants, audits, mapping and manual
  evidence collection?
- How many employees maintain this work?
- What delays, rejected shipments or duplicated investigations occur?
- What would losing or delaying market access cost?
- Who receives the measurable financial benefit?
- Who currently owns the budget for solving it?

Do not price from the total value of Thailand's exports or Vietnam's coffee
sector. Price from the specific organisation's addressable problem and AAB's
attributable contribution to solving it.

### 3. Compare real alternatives

The EUDR compliance market uses negotiated enterprise pricing. Plot, hectare
and supplier volume are more meaningful pricing dimensions than user seats.

For each engagement, compare the buyer's actual alternatives:

- Consultants and manual evidence collection
- Certification and audit providers
- GIS and satellite-analysis services
- Traceability and compliance software
- Internal staff and spreadsheets
- Doing nothing and accepting the regulatory risk

AAB's price must be competitive with these alternatives while reflecting its
genuine advantages — governed evidence chains, honest gap disclosure, traceable
provenance, and defensibility under regulatory challenge.

### 4. Test willingness to pay

Before pricing any engagement, speak to at least:

- Two government or national bodies
- Three exporters or commercial operators
- Two cooperatives or industry groups
- Two research institutions
- One development funder

Show each the same bounded engagement and ask:

- Would your organisation fund this?
- Which budget would pay for it?
- What approval threshold applies?
- Would AUD 25,000, AUD 50,000, AUD 100,000 or AUD 250,000 require different
  procurement processes?
- Which outcomes would justify renewal?
- What would make the proposal unacceptable?

Do not begin with "What would you like to pay?" Provide defined scopes and test
specific price points.

## What AAB can charge for first

The first commercial offer should be a paid institutional discovery and
controlled pilot. Every offer must clearly state that it does not produce
regulatory approval or assured compliance.

| Component | Charging basis |
|---|---|
| Institutional discovery | Fixed fee |
| Country readiness and sovereignty assessment | Fixed fee |
| Controlled pilot | Fixed scope and duration |
| Country environment setup | One-time implementation fee |
| Annual platform operation | Annual base fee |
| Domain entitlement | Annual fee per domain |
| Scale | Plots, hectares, suppliers, evidence volume or packages |
| Integrations | One-time plus maintenance |
| Enhanced support | SLA tier |
| Satellite and licensed datasets | Transparent pass-through or usage fee |
| Specialist services | Day rate or scoped project |

Per-user pricing should not be the primary model. It discourages smallholder
and scientific participation and poorly reflects workload or value.

## The three initial offers

**Offer 1 — Paid institutional discovery**
A bounded engagement to understand the institution's supply chain, evidence
landscape, regulatory exposure, and readiness for a controlled pilot. Fixed
scope, fixed fee, clear deliverables. Does not produce compliance outputs.

**Offer 2 — Controlled country or supply-chain pilot**
A scoped deployment against a specific commodity, a defined set of plots and
operators, and a specific regulatory framework. Produces a governed evidence
package — not a compliance certificate. Fixed scope and duration. Priced after
the discovery engagement has provided the necessary data.

**Offer 3 — Production deployment**
Price determined only after the pilot. The pilot provides the missing data:
actual labour, infrastructure, evidence volumes, buyer value, support burden,
and willingness to pay. After two or three paid pilots, AAB can establish a
defensible rate card.

## Who pays

The payer, contracting party, tenant owner, and beneficiary must remain
separate records. In a typical country deployment:

- The country sponsor pays for sovereign infrastructure and governance
- Exporters pay for commercial SCS domain use
- Research institutions pay for AGR domain participation — or receive funded access
- Exporters, government, or development partners sponsor cooperative access
- Third-party development funders may finance a national or regional programme

## Subsidies must be explicit

If a cooperative receives AUD 80,000 of service for AUD 5,000, the remaining
AUD 75,000 must be recorded as a government, exporter, grant, or strategic
subsidy — not as an unexplained discount. Every subsidy appears explicitly in
the commercial agreement.

## The pricing worksheet

Before quoting any engagement, complete a one-page pricing worksheet:

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| Engagement scope | What is being delivered |
| Current maturity and limitations | What AAB cannot yet do |
| Number of organisations | Parties involved |
| Number of suppliers and plots | Scale of evidence |
| Expected evidence volume | Deforestation, custody, registry records |
| Domains and capabilities involved | AGR, SCS, specific capabilities |
| Implementation effort | Labour estimate |
| External data and service costs | Satellite, registry, mapping |
| Annual operating cost | Infrastructure and support |
| Support commitment | SLA and response terms |
| Customer's current alternative cost | What they spend today |
| Measurable value or risk addressed | What AAB attributably contributes |
| Minimum sustainable price | Cost floor calculation |
| Proposed price | The actual number |
| Discount or subsidy source | Explicit — never unexplained |
| Pilot success and renewal criteria | What good looks like |

## What must not happen

- Do not publish a country price list before completing paid pilots
- Do not embed specific dollar amounts in CAP-21 or any platform capability
- Do not anchor an institution to a price range that has no cost evidence behind it
- Do not imply that purchasing access constitutes compliance, admission, or
  production authorisation
- Do not allow commercial pressure to bypass governance admission, implementation
  readiness, or regulatory review

## Relationship to CAP-20 and CAP-21

CAP-20 (Country Capability Catalogue and Selection) governs what is visible,
what is requestable, and what is truthfully disclosed about each capability's
maturity and availability.

CAP-21 (Commercial Agreement and Entitlement Management) stores entitlement
scope, term, sponsor, and status — with a reference to an external commercial
agreement where the actual financial terms live. CAP-21 does not store prices.
It enforces entitlement status: active or not.

The platform enforces what an organisation is entitled to use. The commercial
agreement records what they paid. These are separate documents with separate
lifecycles.
